## Article 136 of the Constitution of India: Special Leave to Appeal by the Supreme Court

### Exact Article Text and What it Says

**Article 136 of the Constitution of India, 1950**, titled "Special leave to appeal by the Supreme Court," states:

"(1) Notwithstanding anything in this Chapter, the Supreme Court may, in its discretion, grant special leave to appeal from any judgment, decree, determination, sentence or order in any cause or matter passed or made by any Court or tribunal in the territory of India.
(2) Nothing in clause (1) shall apply to any judgment, determination, sentence or order passed or made by any Court or tribunal constituted by or under any law relating to the Armed Forces."

In essence, Article 136 grants the Supreme Court of India an extraordinary and discretionary power to hear appeals from any court or tribunal within India, except those related to the Armed Forces. This power is not a right of appeal for a litigant but a special privilege that the Supreme Court may choose to grant in exceptional circumstances.

### Historical Background and Significance

The genesis of Article 136 can be traced to Section 205 of the Government of India Act, 1935, which provided for appeals
to the Federal Court. During the Constituent Assembly debates, particularly on Draft Article 112 (which became Article
136), Dr. B.R. Ambedkar championed a broad and unfettered discretion for the Supreme Court. The framers intended Article
136 to be a "safety valve" in the judicial system, allowing the Supreme Court to intervene and correct gross injustices
or miscarriages of law that might not be addressable through ordinary appellate channels. The deliberate broad wording
ensures its applicability across various legal domains. The exclusion of military tribunals under Clause (2) was a
conscious decision, primarily influenced by the Defence Ministry, to maintain discipline within the armed forces, though
it was met with some opposition during the debates.

### Scope and Nature of Power

Article 136 confers a plenary, residuary, and discretionary power on the Supreme Court. It is not an appeal as a matter
of right but a special power exercised only in exceptional cases. The Supreme Court has described this power as an
"untrammeled reservoir of power incapable of being confined to definitional bounds," which cannot be curtailed by any
legislation unless the Article itself is amended. This wide scope allows the Supreme Court to entertain appeals in any
cause or matter—be it civil, criminal, constitutional, taxation, labour, or administrative—against decisions of any
court or tribunal in India, with the sole exception of military tribunals. The Court can even delve into questions of
fact, law, or mixed questions of fact and law, though it generally exercises restraint in re-evaluating factual
findings.
