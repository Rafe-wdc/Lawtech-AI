#### *P. S. R. Sadhanantham v. Arunachalam & Anr., 1980 AIR 856* (DB ID: 6943)

This case primarily deals with the locus standi of a private party to file an SLP against an acquittal, and the broad discretionary power of the Supreme Court under Article 136.

### Key Legal Issues
-   Scope of jurisdiction under Article 136 of the Constitution.
-   Whether a private party (brother of the deceased) can invoke Article 136 to appeal against an acquittal when the government has not.
-   Whether the procedure followed by the Supreme Court in disposing of an SLP is consistent with Article 21 of the Constitution.

### Facts
The petitioner was acquitted by the High Court of charges under Sections 302 and 148 IPC. The brother of the deceased,
not the State or the first informant, filed an SLP under Article 136 against the acquittal. The Supreme Court granted
leave, heard the appeal, and ultimately allowed it, setting aside the High Court's judgment and restoring the conviction
and sentence imposed by the trial court. The petitioner then filed a writ petition under Article 32, challenging the
grant of special leave to the brother of the deceased.

### Detailed Narrative
The petitioner contended that Article 136 did not empower the grant of special leave to the brother of the deceased, and that the Court's action violated Article 21. It was also argued that an antecedent right of appeal must exist before the Court can grant special leave.

### Court Observations
The Court held that justice is outraged when a guilty criminal escapes punishment. Article 136 does not confer a right
of appeal on a party but grants wide discretionary power to the Supreme Court to interfere in suitable cases. This power
is extraordinary and its limit, when chasing injustice, is the sky itself. The Court emphasized that Article 136 is of
composite structure, being both power and procedure, and that fair procedure is implicit in the special leave process,
meeting the demands of Article 21. While generally the Court may not grant leave to one who is not eo nomine a party on
record, a wider connotation of "standing" is necessary for Article 136 to fulfill its mission, especially when
bureaucratic indifference or politicization might lead to the State's refusal to appeal. However, access to Article 136
cannot be permitted for private revenge or vendetta. The Court should entertain an SLP by a private party only when
public interest justifies an appeal against acquittal and the State's inaction is not bona fide.

### Judgment & Direction
The petition was dismissed, affirming the Supreme Court's power to entertain an SLP by a private party in cases of public interest, even against an acquittal, under the broad ambit of Article 136.

#### *Shiva Jute Baling Ltd. v. Hindley & Co. Ltd., 1955 AIR 464* (DB ID: 399)

This judgment clarifies the procedure to be followed after special leave is granted under Article 136, particularly concerning the role of the High Court and the "admission" of the appeal.

### Key Legal Issues
-   Procedure to be followed after the grant of special leave under Article 136 of the Constitution.
-   Applicability of Order XLV of the Civil Procedure Code and Supreme Court Rules (Order XIII, rules 8, 9, 12, and 13) to appeals under Article 136.
-   Whether a formal motion in the High Court for "admission" of the appeal is necessary when special leave has been granted by the Supreme Court.

### Facts
The Supreme Court granted special leave to appeal against a judgment of the Calcutta High Court. The petitioners furnished the required security. However, the Registrar of the High Court did not issue a notice of admission of appeal, and the appellants did not move the High Court for its admission, following the High Court's practice. The respondents then moved both the High Court and the Supreme Court, complaining of default by the appellants in prosecuting the appeal.

### Detailed Narrative
The respondents sought action under Rule 13 of Order XIII of the Supreme Court Rules for rescinding the special leave. The High Court's practice required a formal application for admission of the appeal even after the Supreme Court granted special leave. The appellants argued that they were not responsible for the delay as the High Court's Registrar had not issued the notice of admission.

### Court Observations
The Court held that after special leave is granted under Article 136, the Supreme Court Registrar transmits a certified
copy of the order to the appealed court or tribunal. Rule 9 of Order XIII of the Supreme Court Rules enjoins the
appealed court to act in accordance with Order XLV of the Civil Procedure Code, so far as applicable. However, the
application of Order XLV is restricted. Once security is furnished and other deposits are made, the formality of
"admission" envisaged by Rule 8 of Order XLV of the Civil Procedure Code is unnecessary because the order granting
special leave itself operates as an admission of the appeal as soon as the conditions are complied with. Appeals under
Article 136 stand on a different footing from appeals granted by a High Court certificate. In cases where special leave
has been granted by the Supreme Court, it is not necessary for the appellant to move the High Court for formal admission
of the appeal. The admission is regarded as final upon compliance with the conditions in the special leave order, and it
becomes the Registrar's duty to issue notice of admission.

### Judgment & Direction
The application by the respondents to rescind the special leave was dismissed. The Court clarified that the order granting special leave itself acts as an admission of the appeal upon compliance with its conditions, and no further formal admission by the High Court is required.
