Dealing with a criminal case in India involves a structured legal process governed primarily by the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), 2023, which replaced the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, and the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023, which replaced the Indian Penal Code, 1860. The Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam (BSA), 2023, replaced the Indian Evidence Act, 1872. These new laws came into effect on July 1, 2024.

The process generally unfolds through several stages:

### 1. Registration of First Information Report (FIR)

-   **Initiation:** The criminal justice process typically begins with the registration of a First Information Report (FIR) under Section 173 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (BNSS). An FIR is the police record of the initial information received about a cognizable offence. A cognizable offence is one where the police can arrest and initiate an investigation without a warrant.
-   **Mode of Filing:** Information relating to a cognizable offence can be given orally or by electronic communication. If given orally, it must be reduced to writing and read to the informant, who then signs it. BNSS mandates that FIRs must be registered online, ensuring transparency and reducing delays. Victims can file complaints electronically.
-   **Zero FIR:** The BNSS explicitly recognizes the concept of a "Zero FIR," allowing a complaint to be registered at any police station, irrespective of territorial jurisdiction. This FIR is then transferred to the appropriate police station.
-   **Mandatory Registration:** Police are duty-bound to register an FIR if the information discloses the commission of a cognizable offence. Refusal to register an FIR can lead to disciplinary action against the officer.

### 2. Investigation by Police

-   **Commencement:** Once an FIR is lodged, the police commence their investigation under BNSS. The investigation involves collecting facts, evidence, and materials related to the offence to ascertain if an offence has been committed and who is responsible.
-   **Process:** This includes visiting the crime scene, collecting evidence, examining witnesses, conducting searches and seizures, and arresting the accused if necessary. BNSS allows video recording of statements to prevent coercion.
-   **Timelines:** BNSS imposes stricter timelines for investigation. It must be completed within 60 days for offences punishable with up to 10 years of imprisonment and within 90 days for offences punishable with death or life imprisonment. Victims have the right to receive updates on the investigation.
-   **Forensic Evidence:** For offences punishable with seven years of imprisonment or more, forensic evidence collection by a forensic team at the crime scene is mandatory, and the process must be recorded.

### 3. Arrest and Custody

-   **Arrest:** If the investigation reveals sufficient evidence, the police may arrest the accused.
-   **Custody:** The Constitution and BNSS prohibit detention in police custody beyond 24 hours without a Magistrate's order. The Magistrate can extend police custody up to 15 days, which can be authorised in parts during the initial 40 or 60 days of the total 60 or 90 days of judicial custody. Overall detention cannot exceed 60 or 90 days, depending on the offence.

### 4. Bail

-   **Definition:** Bail, as defined under Section 2(1)(b) of BNSS, is the release of a person accused or suspected of an offence from legal custody upon certain conditions, usually involving a bond or bail bond.
-   **Types of Bail:**
    -   **Bailable Offences:** For bailable offences, bail is a matter of right upon furnishing a bail bond or personal bond. Section 478 of BNSS deals with bail in bailable offences.
    -   **Non-Bailable Offences:** For non-bailable offences, bail is at the discretion of the court, considering factors like the nature and gravity of the offence, the evidence, and the likelihood of the accused absconding.
    -   **Anticipatory Bail:** Section 482 of BNSS allows a person apprehending arrest for a non-bailable offence to apply to the High Court or Sessions Court for directions for release on bail in the event of arrest.
-   **Undertrial Prisoners:** Section 479 of BNSS provides for bail to undertrial prisoners. A person not accused of an offence punishable with death or life imprisonment must be released on bail if detained for half the maximum punishment, or one-third if a first-time offender. However, this provision does not apply if the undertrial is facing multiple charges.
-   **Indigent Persons:** BNSS allows indigent individuals accused of bailable offences to be released on a bail bond without surety if they cannot provide bail within a week.

### 5. Filing of Charge Sheet/Final Report

-   **Submission:** After completing the investigation, the police submit a "police report" to the Magistrate, which can be a charge sheet or a closure report. A charge sheet is defined under Section 193 of BNSS as the final report prepared by a police officer.
-   **Contents:** The charge sheet includes collected evidence, witness statements, the accused's criminal history (if any), and other documentary evidence.
-   **Timelines:** BNSS imposes stricter timelines for charge sheet submission. For serious offences punishable with life imprisonment or death, the charge sheet must be filed within 90 days. For offences punishable with up to 10 years, the timeline is 60 days.

### 6. Cognizance by Magistrate and Committal

-   **Cognizance:** Upon receiving the charge sheet, the Magistrate takes cognizance of the offence.
-   **Committal:** For grave offences exclusively triable by a Court of Sessions (e.g., murder, rape, dacoity, offences punishable with death, life imprisonment, or imprisonment exceeding seven years), the Magistrate commits the case to the Sessions Court. BNSS prescribes a 90-day period for commitments to Sessions Courts.

### 7. Framing of Charges

-   **Court's Role:** The court examines the evidence and decides which charges to formally bring against the accused.
-   **Procedure:** Section 251 of BNSS deals with the framing of charges. The charge must be precise, stating the specific offence and its particulars (time, place, person).
-   **Timelines:** Charges should be framed within 60 days of the first hearing on such charges.
-   **Discharge:** If the court finds insufficient grounds to proceed after examining the evidence and hearing arguments, the accused may be discharged under Section 250 of BNSS before charges are framed.

### 8. Trial Process

-   **Types of Trials:** BNSS outlines procedures for different types of trials:
    -   **Summons Trial:** For petty crimes, with no formal charge framing required.
    -   **Warrant Trial:** For offences punishable with more than 2 years of imprisonment, involving strict evidence and witness examination.
    -   **Sessions Trial:** Handled by the Sessions Court for grave offences, involving detailed witness and cross-examination. The prosecution is conducted by a Public Prosecutor.
-   **Plea:** The accused is informed of the charges and asked to plead guilty or claim trial.
-   **Evidence:**
    -   **Prosecution Evidence:** Prosecution witnesses testify first.
    -   **Defence Evidence:** Defence witnesses are examined next.
    -   **Electronic Mode:** Trials, inquiries, and proceedings may be held in electronic mode, and witnesses may be examined through audio-video electronic means.
-   **Examination of Accused:** The accused is also examined by the court.

### 9. Final Arguments and Judgment

-   **Arguments:** After witness examination, both sides present their final arguments.
-   **Judgment:** The judge then delivers the judgment, which can be an acquittal (if evidence is insufficient) or a conviction (if guilt is proven beyond doubt).
-   **Timelines:** Judgment must be delivered within 30 days of the completion of arguments, extendable up to 60 days.

### 10. Sentencing

-   If convicted, the accused must serve the sentence unless a stay order is obtained from a higher court or a pardon/commutation is granted. BNSS introduces community service as a form of punishment for certain petty offences.

### 11. Appeal Process

-   **Right to Appeal:** Appeal is a statutory right, not an inherent one. If dissatisfied with the verdict, the accused or victim can file an appeal under BNSS.
-   **Hierarchy of Appeals:** Appeals generally follow a hierarchy: Sessions Court → High Court → Supreme Court.
-   **BNSS Provisions:** Chapter XXXI of BNSS (Sections 413 to 435) deals with appeals.
    -   **Appeal against Conviction:** An accused convicted by a Sessions Court has the right to appeal to the High Court under Section 455 of BNSS.
    -   **Appeal against Acquittal:** The State Government or victim can appeal against an acquittal under Section 419 of BNSS.
    -   **Appeal for Sentence Enhancement:** The State Government can appeal for a stricter sentence under Section 418 of BNSS if the punishment awarded is inadequate.
    -   **Appeal to Supreme Court:** If a High Court reverses an acquittal and convicts a person, sentencing them to death, life imprisonment, or ten years or more, the accused may appeal to the Supreme Court.
-   **Timelines for Appeals:** Time limits for appeals are stricter under BNSS than under the previous CrPC. An appeal by the State Government must be filed within six months, and by others within sixty days.

### Key Legal Provisions

-   **Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), 2023:** This Act governs the procedure for criminal investigations, trials, and related matters. It replaced the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, and came into force on July 1, 2024.
