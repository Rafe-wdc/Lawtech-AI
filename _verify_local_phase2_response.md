## Applicable Offences under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023

1. The actions described, involving unauthorized access to a fintech company's servers, theft of sensitive personal and financial data, source code, and its subsequent sale on the dark web, attract several provisions of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (hereinafter referred to as "BNS, 2023"). The primary offences are as follows:

2. **Section 303 (Theft):** The act of dishonestly taking any movable property out of the possession of any person without that person's consent constitutes theft. The stolen data, including Aadhaar records, PAN data, bank account details, credit card information, and source code, being stored on servers and having value, is considered movable property for the purposes of this section. The unauthorized access and exfiltration of this data clearly fall within the definition of theft.

3. **Section 318 (Mischief by injury to computer resource):** This is a specific offence directly applicable to the act
of hacking. Whoever commits mischief by doing any act in respect of any computer resource, intending to cause or knowing
that they are likely to cause wrongful loss or damage to the public or to any person, shall be punished. The
unauthorized access to the fintech company's servers and the theft of data inherently cause wrongful loss to the company
and the individuals whose data was stolen.

4. **Section 378 (Cheating):** The sale of the stolen data on the dark web involves deceiving the purchasers and fraudulently inducing them to deliver money. Furthermore, this act is part of a larger scheme that causes wrongful loss to the data subjects and the fintech company.

5. **Section 380 (Cheating by personation):** The theft of identity information such as Aadhaar records and PAN data is a precursor to cheating by personation. The hacker, by possessing and selling this data, enables its use for impersonating the data subjects for fraudulent purposes.

6. **Section 405 (Receiving stolen property):** Any individual who dishonestly receives or retains the stolen data from the dark web, knowing or having reason to believe it is stolen property, would be liable under this section. This applies to the buyers of the stolen data.

## Provisions of the Information Technology Act

7. The Information Technology Act, 2000 (hereinafter referred to as "IT Act, 2000") contains specific provisions to deal with cybercrimes of this nature, which would be applied in conjunction with the BNS, 2023.

8. **Section 43 (Penalty and compensation for damage to computer, computer system, etc.):** This section establishes civil liability. Any person who, without permission of the owner, accesses or secures access to a computer system, or downloads, copies, or extracts any data or computer database, is liable to pay damages by way of compensation to the person so affected. The hacker's actions are a direct violation of this provision.

9. **Section 65 (Tampering with computer source documents):** This section is directly applicable to the theft of the fintech company's source code. It penalizes anyone who knowingly or intentionally conceals, destroys, or alters any computer source code required to be kept or maintained by law.

10. **Section 66 (Computer related offences):** This is the primary penal provision under the IT Act, 2000. It makes any act mentioned in Section 43 a criminal offence if done dishonestly or fraudulently. The hacker's unauthorized access and data theft, being committed with dishonest intent, are punishable with imprisonment for a term which may extend to three years or with a fine which may extend to five lakh rupees, or with both.

11. **Section 66B (Punishment for dishonestly receiving stolen computer resource or communication device):** This provision specifically targets the buyers of the stolen data on the dark web. Anyone who dishonestly receives or retains any stolen computer resource (which includes the data) shall be punished under this section.

12. **Section 66C (Punishment for identity theft):** This is a critical charge in this case. The theft of Aadhaar records, PAN data, bank account details, and credit card information for fraudulent use constitutes identity theft, which is punishable with imprisonment for a term which may extend to three years and also a fine.

13. **Section 66D (Punishment for cheating by personation by using computer resource):** This section complements the BNS provision for cheating by personation. It penalizes anyone who, by means of a computer resource or a communication device, cheats by personation. The sale and subsequent use of the stolen identity data would fall under this offence.

14. **Section 72A (Punishment for disclosure of information in breach of lawful contract):** If the investigation reveals that the hacker was an employee, consultant, or any person who had access to the data under a lawful contract, this section would apply. It penalizes the disclosure of personal information, secured during the provision of services, with the intent to cause wrongful loss or wrongful gain.

## Investigation Procedure

15. The investigation into a complex cybercrime, such as the unauthorized access to the fintech company's servers and the subsequent data theft, will be conducted in accordance with the provisions of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 (CrPC) (and its successor, the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023), read with the specific provisions of the Information Technology Act, 2000. The key procedural steps are as follows:

16. **Filing of First Information Report (FIR):** The first step is the registration of an FIR by the fintech company under Section 154 of the CrPC. The FIR will detail the nature of the unauthorized access, the types of data stolen (Aadhaar records, PAN data, bank account details, credit card information, source code), the suspected timeframe of the breach, and the discovery of the data being sold on the dark web.

17. **Transfer to a Specialized Unit:** Given the technical complexity of the case, upon registration of the FIR, the investigation will likely be transferred from the local police station to a specialized unit such as a Cyber Crime Cell or Cyber Police Station, which possesses the necessary technical expertise and resources.

18. **Jurisdiction:** Cybercrimes often transcend geographical boundaries. As per Section 75 of the IT Act, 2000, the provisions of the Act apply to any offence or contravention committed outside India by any person if the act or conduct involves a computer, computer system, or computer network located in India. Therefore, Indian authorities have jurisdiction to investigate and prosecute the hacker, irrespective of their physical location.

19. **Search and Seizure:** The investigating agency will conduct search and seizure operations to gather evidence. This is governed by the CrPC and Section 80 of the IT Act, 2000, which empowers a police officer, not below the rank of Inspector, to enter any public place and search and arrest without a warrant any person reasonably suspected of committing an offence under the Act. This includes seizing servers, computers, storage devices, and other relevant electronic equipment.

20. **Collection of Evidence:** The investigating team will focus on collecting all forms of digital and traditional evidence. This involves securing the compromised servers, collecting log files, imaging storage devices, and gathering information from the dark web marketplace. Statements of the fintech company's employees (IT staff, security personnel) will be recorded under Section 161 of the CrPC.

21. **Arrest of the Accused:** Once the identity of the hacker is established and there is sufficient evidence of their involvement, they will be arrested in accordance with the procedure laid down in Section 41 of the CrPC.

22. **Forensic Analysis:** All seized electronic devices and data will be sent to a Cyber Forensic Laboratory for detailed analysis to trace the origin of the attack, understand the methodology, recover deleted data, and link the accused to the crime.

23. **Filing of Final Report (Charge Sheet):** Upon completion of the investigation, if sufficient evidence is gathered
against the accused, the investigating officer will file a final report (charge sheet) under Section 173 of the CrPC
before the competent court, outlining the offences committed and the evidence collected. As per Rule 19 of The Uttar
Pradesh Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Rules, 2024, the investigating officer is also obligated to inform the victim (the
fintech company) about the progress of the investigation.

## Digital Evidence Collection

24. The collection of digital evidence is a critical and sensitive phase of the investigation, requiring strict adherence to forensic procedures to ensure the evidence's integrity and admissibility in court. The process involves the following key stages:

25. **Identification of Evidence Sources:** The first step is to identify all potential sources of digital evidence. In this case, these would include:
a) The compromised servers of the fintech company.
b) Network devices such as firewalls, routers, and intrusion detection systems.
c) System, application, and security logs from all relevant infrastructure.
d) Workstations of employees with privileged access.
e) Backup tapes and other storage media.
f) Data from the dark web marketplace where the stolen information was listed for sale.
g) Any communication devices or computer systems belonging to the suspect(s) once identified.

26. **Preservation of Evidence:** To prevent any alteration or destruction of data, the crime scene (the digital environment) must be secured. This involves isolating the affected servers from the network to stop ongoing data exfiltration or tampering by the hacker. All subsequent analysis must be performed on a forensic copy, not the original evidence.

27. **Acquisition of Evidence:** This is the process of collecting the identified digital evidence.
a) **Forensic Imaging:** A bit-for-bit copy (forensic image or clone) of the storage media from the servers and other devices is created using specialized hardware (write-blockers) and software. Write-blockers prevent any data from being written to the original device during the imaging process.
b) **Volatile Data Collection:** Before powering down any system, volatile data stored in the Random Access Memory (RAM) is collected. This data, which includes running processes, active network connections, and logged-in users, is lost when the system is turned off and can provide crucial real-time information about the attack.
c) **Log Collection:** All relevant logs (system, security, firewall, application, database) are collected and preserved. These logs can help create a timeline of the hacker's activities.

28. **Documentation and Chain of Custody:** Every step of the evidence collection process must be meticulously documented. This includes:
a) **Hashing:** A cryptographic hash value (e.g., SHA-256) is generated for the original data and the forensic copy. These unique digital fingerprints must match to prove that the copy is an exact and unaltered replica of the original.
b) **Chain of Custody:** A detailed log is maintained to record the entire lifecycle of the evidence. It documents who collected the evidence, when and where it was collected, who has handled it since, and where it has been stored. An unbroken chain of custody is essential to prove in court that the evidence has not been tampered with.

## Preservation of Electronic Evidence

29. The preservation of electronic evidence is of paramount importance to ensure its integrity, authenticity, and admissibility in a court of law. Any failure in the preservation process can render the evidence inadmissible. The following procedures are critical:

30. **Isolation of Systems:** The first and most immediate step is to isolate the compromised servers and other affected systems from the company's network and the internet. This action prevents the hacker from remotely accessing the systems to destroy evidence, cause further damage, or continue data exfiltration. It also preserves the volatile data in its current state for immediate collection.

31. **Creation of Forensic Images:** All analysis must be conducted on a forensic copy, never on the original evidence. A bit-for-bit forensic image (also known as a bit-stream copy) of all relevant storage media (server hard drives, SSDs, etc.) must be created. This process creates an exact duplicate of the original media, including deleted files, file fragments in unallocated space, and other latent data, thereby preserving the original evidence in its pristine state.

32. **Use of Write-Blockers:** During the creation of forensic images, hardware or software write-blockers must be used. These devices prevent any data from being written to the original storage media, thus ensuring that the acquisition process itself does not alter the evidence in any way.

33. **Hashing for Integrity Verification:** To prove that the forensic copy is an exact and unaltered replica of the original, cryptographic hash values (using algorithms like SHA-256) must be generated for both the original media and the forensic image. The hash values act as unique digital fingerprints. If the values match, it provides mathematical proof of the evidence's integrity at the time of collection. This process must be documented.

34. **Secure Storage and Chain of Custody:** The original evidence devices and all forensic copies must be stored in a secure, tamper-proof location with restricted access. A meticulous Chain of Custody log must be maintained from the moment of seizure. This log must record every individual who handles the evidence, the date and time of access or transfer, and the purpose for which it was handled. An unbroken chain of custody is essential to defend against allegations of evidence tampering.

## Role of Cyber Forensic Laboratory

35. The Cyber Forensic Laboratory plays a pivotal role in the investigation by providing the scientific and technical analysis required to understand the crime and produce admissible evidence. Its functions are crucial for building a case against the perpetrator.

36. **Scientific Analysis of Evidence:** The primary role of the laboratory is to conduct an in-depth forensic examination of the digital evidence, including the forensic images of the fintech company's servers, network logs, and any devices seized from suspects. This analysis involves:
a) Recovering deleted files, hidden data, and encrypted information that may be relevant to the investigation.
b) Analyzing system logs, application logs, and network traffic data to reconstruct the timeline of the attack, identify the methods used (modus operandi), and trace the path of data exfiltration.
c) Identifying and analyzing any malware, rootkits, or other malicious tools used by the hacker to gain access and steal the data.
d) Tracing the digital footprints of the attacker, such as IP addresses, email accounts, or cryptocurrency wallets used in the sale of data on the dark web.
e) Examining the stolen source code to identify vulnerabilities that were exploited or to check for any malicious code inserted by the hacker.

37. **Data Reconstruction and Link Analysis:** Forensic experts reconstruct the sequence of events to create a clear narrative of the cybercrime. They perform link analysis to connect the digital artifacts found on the compromised systems to the suspect and the data being sold on the dark web.

38. **Preparation of Expert Report:** Following the analysis, the laboratory prepares a detailed expert report. This document outlines the evidence examined, the forensic tools and methodologies used, the findings of the analysis, and the expert's conclusions. This report serves as a critical piece of evidence for the prosecution.

39. **Providing Expert Testimony:** Forensic experts from the laboratory are often required to testify in court. They explain their technical findings to the court in a clear and understandable manner, establish the authenticity and integrity of the electronic evidence, and defend their conclusions under cross-examination. Their testimony is vital for the court to appreciate the technical nuances of the case.

## Admissibility of Electronic Evidence under Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam

40. The admissibility of electronic evidence collected during the investigation is governed by the provisions of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 (hereinafter referred to as "BSA, 2023"). For the digital evidence in this caseâsuch as server logs, forensic images of hard drives, network traffic data, and data from the dark webâto be admissible in court, strict compliance with the statutory requirements is mandatory.

41. **Section 61 of the BSA, 2023** provides that the contents of documents may be proved by either primary or secondary evidence. Electronic records are treated as documents under the BSA, 2023.

42. **Section 62 of the BSA, 2023** defines primary evidence as the document itself produced for the inspection of the Court. In the context of electronic records, the original media on which the data is stored (e.g., the original server hard drive) could be considered primary evidence. However, producing original electronic records is often impractical and risky.

43. **Section 64 and Section 65 of the BSA, 2023** lay down the special provisions for proving the contents of
electronic records. These sections are the cornerstone for the admissibility of electronic evidence. Section 65 states
that any information contained in an electronic record, which is printed on paper or stored/copied in optical or
magnetic media produced by a computer (referred to as computer output), shall be deemed a document and is admissible as
evidence of the contents of the original, provided certain conditions are met.

44. The conditions for admissibility under **Section 65(2) of the BSA, 2023** are as follows:
a) The computer output containing the information was produced by the computer during a period when it was used regularly to store or process information for regular activities by a person having lawful control over its use.
b) During the said period, information of the kind contained in the electronic record was regularly fed into the computer in the ordinary course of activities.
c) The computer was operating properly throughout the material period. If it was not, the malfunction must not have been such as to affect the electronic record or the accuracy of its contents.
d) The information contained in the computer output reproduces or is derived from the information fed into the computer in the ordinary course of activities.

45. **Certificate Requirement under Section 65(4) of the BSA, 2023:** The most critical procedural requirement for admitting a computer output as evidence is the submission of a certificate. This certificate must:
a) Identify the electronic record containing the statement and describe the manner in which it was produced.
b) Provide particulars of the device involved in the production of the electronic record.
c) Certify that the conditions mentioned in Section 65(2) are satisfied in relation to the information and the device.
d) Be signed by a person occupying a responsible official position in relation to the operation of the relevant device or the management of the relevant activities (e.g., the head of the IT department of the fintech company or the forensic expert).

46. In this case, every piece of electronic evidence, from the server logs of the fintech company to the forensic analysis report from the laboratory, must be accompanied by the requisite certificate under Section 65(4) of the BSA, 2023, to ensure its admissibility and to establish its authenticity and integrity before the court.
