Here are the explanations for Sections 64, 65, and 66 of The Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023:

**Section 64: Rules as to notice to produce**

This section deals with the requirement of giving notice before admitting secondary evidence of a document. Secondary evidence of the contents of a document (as referred to in clause (a) of section 60) cannot be presented unless the party intending to use this secondary evidence has first given notice to the opposing party (or their advocate/representative) to produce the original document. The notice must be as prescribed by law, or if no specific notice is prescribed, then a notice that the Court deems reasonable given the circumstances.

However, this notice is not required in certain situations, including:
-   When the document to be proven is itself a notice.
-   When, due to the nature of the case, the adverse party would reasonably expect to be required to produce the document.
-   When it is evident or proven that the adverse party obtained the original document through fraud or force.
-   When the adverse party or their agent has the original document in court.
-   When the adverse party or their agent has admitted that the document is lost.
-   When the person in possession of the document is beyond the reach of, or not subject to, the Court's process.
-   In any other situation where the Court decides to waive the notice requirement.

**Section 65: Proof of signature and handwriting of person alleged to have signed or written document produced**

If a document is claimed to have been signed or written, either entirely or partially, by a specific person, then the signature or the handwriting of the part alleged to be in that person's hand must be proven to be indeed in their handwriting.

**Section 66: Proof as to electronic signature**

Unless the electronic signature is a secure electronic signature, if it is alleged that an electronic signature of a subscriber was affixed to an electronic record, then it must be proven that the electronic signature is indeed that of the subscriber.

The Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (BNSS) significantly overhauls and consolidates provisions related to bail, building upon existing frameworks while introducing new aspects. This response synthesizes the key bail provisions under the BNSS, drawing from its statutory text and relevant judicial interpretations.

## Statutory Basis for Bail under the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023

The BNSS defines and regulates various aspects of bail, including its definition, types of offences, general provisions, anticipatory bail, and conditions for release.

### Definition and Classification of Offences

-   **Definition of Bail:** Section 2(b) of the BNSS defines "bail" as "release of a person accused of or suspected of commission of an offence from the custody of law upon certain conditions imposed by an officer or Court on execution by such person of a bond or a bail bond."
-   **Bailable and Non-Bailable Offences:** Section 2(c) distinguishes between "bailable offence" (as listed in the First Schedule or made bailable by law) and "non-bailable offence" (any other offence).

### General Provisions for Bail

-   **Release on Bail for Non-Non-Bailable Offences:** Section 478(1) mandates the release on bail of any person arrested or detained without a warrant, or who appears or is brought before a Court, provided they are not accused of a non-bailable offence and are prepared to give bail.
    -   **Proviso:** If a person is indigent and unable to furnish surety, the officer or Court may discharge them upon executing a bond for their appearance. An inability to furnish bail within a week of arrest can be presumed as indigence.
    -   **Explanation:** This section is subject to other provisions like Section 135(3) and Section 492.
-   **Refusal of Bail for Non-Compliance:** Section 478(2) states that if a person fails to comply with the conditions of a bond regarding attendance, the Court may refuse to release them on bail on a subsequent occasion in the same case.

### Bail in Case of Non-Bailable Offences

The BNSS provides detailed provisions for granting bail in non-bailable offences, with specific restrictions and exceptions.

-   **Conditions for Release:** Section 480(1) outlines when a person accused or suspected of a non-bailable offence can be released on bail by an officer or Court (other than the High Court or Court of Session):
    -   **Restrictions:** Release is prohibited if there are reasonable grounds to believe the person is guilty of an offence punishable with death or life imprisonment (Section 480(1)(i)). Release is also prohibited if the offence is cognizable and the person has prior convictions for offences punishable with death, life imprisonment, or imprisonment for seven years or more, or has been previously convicted on two or more occasions for a cognizable offence punishable with imprisonment for three to seven years (Section 480(1)(ii)).
    -   **Provisos for Exception:**
        -   **Proviso 1:** A person falling under Section 480(1)(i) or (ii) may be released on bail if they are a child, woman, sick, or infirm.
        -   **Proviso 2:** Release may be granted under Section 480(1)(ii) if the Court is satisfied it is just and proper for any special reason.
        -   **Proviso 3:** The need for identification by witnesses or police custody beyond fifteen days is not a sufficient ground to refuse bail if the accused is otherwise entitled and provides an undertaking to comply with Court directions.
        -   **Proviso 4:** For offences punishable with death, life imprisonment, or imprisonment for seven years or more, bail shall not be granted without hearing the Public Prosecutor.
-   **Release Pending Further Inquiry:** Section 480(2) allows for release on bail if, at any stage, there are insufficient grounds for believing the accused committed a non-bailable offence, but sufficient grounds exist for further inquiry.
-   **Conditions for Bail (Offences Punishable with Seven Years or More Imprisonment):** Section 480(3) mandates specific conditions for bail for offences punishable with imprisonment for seven years or more, or under specific Chapters of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (BNS), or abetment/conspiracy/attempt of such offences. These include:
    -   Attending in accordance with the bond.
    -   Not committing a similar offence.
    -   Not inducing, threatening, or promising any person acquainted with the facts to dissuade them from disclosing facts or tampering with evidence.
    -   Other conditions as deemed necessary in the interest of justice.
-   **Recording of Reasons:** Section 480(4) requires the officer or Court releasing a person on bail to record their reasons or special reasons in writing.
-   **Cancellation of Bail:** Section 480(5) empowers any Court that has released a person on bail to direct their arrest and commitment to custody if deemed necessary.
-   **Bail for Trial Delay (Magistrate's Court):** Section 480(6) stipulates that if a trial for a non-bailable offence in a Magistrate's Court is not concluded within sixty days from the first date fixed for evidence, the accused shall be released on bail unless the Magistrate records reasons otherwise.
-   **Bail Pending Judgment:** Section 480(7) provides that if, after the conclusion of a trial and before judgment, the Court believes there are reasonable grounds that the accused is not guilty, the accused shall be released on bail upon executing a bond for appearance to hear the judgment.

### Anticipatory Bail

The BNSS retains the provision for anticipatory bail, allowing individuals to seek pre-arrest bail.

-   **Application for Anticipatory Bail:** Section 482(1) permits any person who has reason to believe they may be arrested for a non-bailable offence to apply to the High Court or Court of Session for a direction for release on bail in the event of arrest.
-   **Conditions for Anticipatory Bail:** Section 482(2) allows the High Court or Court of Session to impose conditions, including:
    -   Availability for police interrogation.
    -   Not inducing, threatening, or promising to dissuade witnesses or tamper with evidence.
    -   Not leaving India without prior Court permission.
    -   Other conditions as per Section 480(3).
-   **Release upon Arrest:** Section 482(3) states that if such a person is arrested without a warrant, they shall be released on bail if prepared to furnish it. If a Magistrate decides to issue a warrant, it shall be a bailable warrant in conformity with the Court's direction.
-   **Exclusions:** Section 482(4) clarifies that anticipatory bail provisions do not apply to arrests for offences under Section 65 and Section 70(2) of the BNS.

### Powers of High Court or Court of Session Regarding Bail

Higher courts have significant powers to grant or modify bail orders.

-   **General Powers:** Section 483(1) empowers the High Court or Court of Session to direct the release of an accused on bail and to set aside or modify conditions imposed by a Magistrate.
    -   **Proviso 1:** For offences exclusively triable by the Court of Session or punishable with life imprisonment, notice of the bail application must be given to the Public Prosecutor, unless impracticable for recorded reasons.
    -   **Proviso 2:** For offences under Section 65 or Section 70(2) of the BNS, notice of the bail application must be given to the Public Prosecutor within fifteen days of receipt.
-   **Presence of Informant:** Section 483(2) mandates the obligatory presence of the informant or their authorized representative when hearing bail applications for offences under Section 65 or Section 70(2) of the BNS.
-   **Arrest and Custody:** Section 483(3) allows the High Court or Court of Session to direct the arrest and commitment to custody of a person released on bail.

### Bail Bond and Sureties

The BNSS elaborates on the nature of bonds and the responsibilities of sureties.

Tenant rights under rent control legislation are multifaceted, encompassing protections against arbitrary eviction, rights related to rent and amenities, and provisions for maintenance and repairs. These rights are established through statutory provisions and have been interpreted and reinforced by various court decisions.

## Statutory Basis for Tenant Rights

The primary statutory framework for tenant rights in rent control legislation provides for:

### Eviction Protection

-   **General Protection:** Tenants are protected from eviction except in accordance with the specific provisions outlined in the Act. A tenant "shall not be evicted except in accordance with the provisions of the Act" (Section 12(1)).
-   **Specific Grounds for Eviction:** Landlords can only initiate eviction proceedings on clearly defined grounds, which may include:
    -   Non-payment of rent.
    -   Bonafide need of the landlord for occupation.
    -   Tenant using the building in a manner that diminishes its value.
    -   Tenant possessing other sufficient accommodation.
    -   Need for reconstruction of the building.
    -   Tenant ceasing to occupy the building for a continuous period of six months without reasonable cause (Section 12(2), 12(3), 12(4), 12(5)).
-   **Right to Contest Eviction:** Tenants have the explicit right to contest any eviction application before the Rent Control Court (Section 12(2)(a), Section 13(1)).
-   **Opportunity to Present Defense:** The Rent Control Court is mandated to provide the tenant with a reasonable opportunity to present their case and show cause against an eviction application (Section 12(2)(b)).
-   **Deposit of Rent to Contest:** To effectively contest an eviction application or an appeal, a tenant is generally required to pay or deposit all admitted arrears of rent and continue to pay or deposit subsequent rent as it becomes due until the proceedings are concluded (Section 13(1)). The Rent Control Court may specify timelines for these deposits, typically not less than 45 days for arrears and two weeks for subsequent rent from the due date (Section 13(2)).
-   **Restoration to Possession:** In cases where a landlord obtains possession of a property but fails to occupy it or vacates it without reasonable cause within stipulated periods, the evicted tenant may have the right to apply for restoration to their previous possession (Section 12(12)).
-   **Protection for Essential Services and Institutions:** Specific protections are in place for tenants engaged in notified essential services, and for buildings let out and used as recognized educational institutions, preventing eviction as long as these conditions persist (Section 12(11)(i), 12(11)(ii)).
-   **Protection for Armed Forces Members/Widows:** Special provisions may exist to allow members of the armed forces or their widows to recover possession for bonafide occupation, subject to specific conditions and documentation (Section 12(11A)).

### Rent and Amenities

-   **Fair Rent Fixation:** The legislation provides mechanisms for the fixation of "fair rent" for tenanted premises (implied by Section 7(1)).
-   **Rent Increase Limitations:** Increases in taxes and cesses by the landlord are generally recoverable from the tenant, but such recovery is often capped, for instance, not exceeding 5% of the original fair rent (Section 7(1)). Disputes concerning rent increases are adjudicated by the Rent Control Court (Section 7(2)).
-   **Protection Against Withholding Amenities:** Landlords are prohibited from cutting off or withholding any amenities that the tenant has been enjoying, without just or sufficient cause (Section 14(1)).
-   **Tenant's Complaint for Withheld Amenities:** If a landlord contravenes the provision regarding amenities, the tenant has the right to file a complaint with the Rent Control Court (Section 14(2)).
-   **Interim Orders for Amenities:** The Rent Control Court has the power to issue interim orders for the immediate restoration of amenities if it is satisfied that they were withheld with the intention of compelling the tenant to vacate or pay enhanced rent (Section 14(3)).
-   **Restoration and Compensation:** If the Court finds that amenities were withheld without just cause, it will order their restoration (Section 14(4)). Tenants may also be awarded compensation if amenities were withheld frivolously or vexatiously (Section 14(5)(b)).

### Maintenance and Repairs

-   **Landlord's Duty for Repairs:** Landlords are generally bound to undertake periodical maintenance and necessary repairs of the building and its associated amenities (Section 18(1)).
-   **Tenant's Right to Repair and Deduct:** If a landlord fails to carry out necessary repairs after receiving notice, the Rent Control Court may authorize the tenant to undertake the repairs and deduct the cost, up to a specified limit, from the rent payable (Section 18(1)). Orders passed by the Rent Control Court regarding repairs are typically final and not subject to appeal (Section 18(2)).

### Other Rights

-   **Notice for Rent Default:** Before initiating eviction proceedings for rent default, a landlord is usually required to issue a registered notice to the tenant, informing them of the default (Section 12(2)(b) proviso).
-   **Time to Rectify Default:** Upon receiving notice of rent default, the tenant is typically given a period, such as 15 days, to pay the arrears, along with any applicable interest and postal charges, to avert eviction proceedings (Section 12(2)(b) proviso).
-   **Time to Vacate After Eviction Order:** An eviction order usually cannot be executed immediately. A period, often one month from the date of the order, is provided during which the tenant can vacate. This period may also allow the tenant to deposit arrears, interest, and costs to potentially set aside the eviction order (Section 12(2)(c)).
-   **Compensation for Frivolous Eviction Applications:** If an eviction application is deemed frivolous or vexatious by the Rent Control Court, the landlord may be directed to pay compensation to the tenant (Section 12(14)).
-   **Continuation of Tenancy:** If an eviction application is dismissed, the tenancy continues on its existing terms and conditions, and the landlord cannot terminate it except on the grounds permitted by the Act (Section 12(15)).
-   **Right to First Option on Reconstructed Building:** Tenants evicted due to the need for reconstruction may have a right to the first option to occupy the reconstructed building at a fair rent (Section 12(4)(iv) third proviso).

## Case Law Interpretations and Applications

Court decisions have further clarified and expanded upon these tenant rights:

### Right to Essential Services

-   In **Mahendra Kumar Rawat v. Radheshyam Gupta and Others** (Rajasthan High Court), the court affirmed that a tenant is entitled to the restoration or a new electricity connection as a basic requirement and right, directing that applications for such connections be dealt with promptly.

### Protection Against Unjust Eviction and Tenancy Rights

-   The Supreme Court in **S. P. Jinadathappa v. R. P. Sharma and Others** upheld the constitutionality of provisions allowing a Controller to select a tenant for a vacant house, deeming it a reasonable restriction in the interest of the general public needing accommodation.
-   The Kerala High Court in **N.P. Pushpangadan and Others v. The Federal Bank Ltd. and Others** clarified that the Securitisation and Reconstruction of Financial Assets and Enforcement of Security Interest Act, 2002, does not override the Kerala Buildings (Lease and Rent Control) Act, 1965, for tenants inducted before the creation of a security interest. Such tenants cannot be summarily evicted and are entitled to make applications to the Debts Recovery Tribunal.
-   In **Neelima Singh v. Ashit Kumar Bhattacharya** (Delhi High Court), the court emphasized that if a tenant's legal heirs inherit tenancy rights, they are protected by the Rent Control Act, and eviction would require statutory grounds. If not, the landlord's remedy would be a civil suit. The court also noted the importance of procedural laws facilitating justice in the continuation of proceedings against legal representatives.
-   The Kerala High Court in **Anilkumar v. Dheevara Education Trust** held that payment of rent to an unauthorized entity, even if mistakenly made, does not constitute due payment to the landlord, and eviction for arrears could proceed unless the arrears are deposited.
-   The Supreme Court in **Babu Manmohan Das Shah & Ors. v. Bishun Das** interpreted that "material alterations" to a rented accommodation, as per the U.P. (Temporary) Control of Rent and Eviction Act, 1947, are sufficient grounds for eviction, even without constructions that substantially diminish the property's value.
-   In **Ashokan P.K. & Latha v. Parakkadavu Grama Panchayath & Ors.** (Kerala High Court), the court ruled that a Panchayat cannot insist on a landlord's consent for renewing a trade license for a tenant who is holding over under the Rent Control Act, as long as the landlord is receiving rent. The landlord's recourse for issues like subletting lies with the Rent Control Authority.

### Fair Rent Fixation and Revisions

-   The Kerala High Court in **Rexine House & Anr. v. Paul Varghese & Ors.** modified fair rent determinations, emphasizing the need for adjustments based on comparable lease deeds and affirming the landlord's right to periodical revision.
-   In **Kunnath Balakrishnan (Died) & Ors. v. Sheenath A. & Ors.** (Kerala High Court), the court highlighted that fair rent fixation should consider various economic factors and comparable premises in the locality. It also noted that issues regarding rent reduction due to subsequent events would require a separate application.

## Remedies for Tenants

Tenants facing violations of their rights under rent control legislation have several avenues for redressal:

-   **Contesting Eviction:** Tenants can contest eviction proceedings in the Rent Control Court, presenting defenses and challenging the landlord's grounds.

Theft is defined as the dishonest moving of any movable property out of the possession of any person without that person's consent, with the intention to take it. This definition is consistent under both the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (BNS) and the Indian Penal Code, 1860 (IPC).

## Statutory Basis

### Definition and General Punishment for Theft

-   **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (BNS):**
    -   **Section 303(1): Theft** defines theft as "dishonestly moving any movable property out of the possession of any person without that person’s consent, with the intention to take it." The section includes explanations regarding things attached to the earth, the effect of severance, causing a thing to move, causing an animal to move, and the nature of consent, along with illustrative scenarios.
    -   **Section 303(2): Punishment for Theft** prescribes punishment of imprisonment of either description for a term which may extend to three years, or with fine, or with both. A proviso allows for community service upon return or restoration of property for first-time convictions where the property value is less than five thousand rupees.

    -   **Section 378: Theft** provides a definition of theft that is substantially similar to BNS Section 303(1), including explanations and illustrations.

This offense was not a distinct category under the IPC but could be prosecuted under existing theft or robbery provisions.
