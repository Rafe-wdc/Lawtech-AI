15. It is submitted that the relationship between the Plaintiff (patient) and the Defendants (doctor and hospital) is one of trust, wherein the Defendants owed a clear and undeniable duty of care to the Plaintiff. This duty required the Defendants to exercise a reasonable degree of skill, knowledge, and care in the treatment of the Plaintiff, consistent with the standards of the medical profession.

16. The Defendant No. 1, being a qualified surgeon, was expected to possess the ordinary skill of his profession and to exercise it with a reasonable degree of care. The act of causing damage to the Plaintiff's bile duct during a standard gallbladder surgery is a clear instance of gross negligence and a failure to adhere to the accepted standards of surgical practice. Such an injury is not an ordinary or acceptable risk associated with the procedure when performed with due care.

17. The principle of *res ipsa loquitur* (the thing speaks for itself) is squarely applicable to the facts of this case. The injury to the bile duct is an event that would not have occurred in the ordinary course of things if the surgeon performing the operation had exercised proper care and skill. The burden of proof, therefore, shifts to the Defendants to show that the injury was not caused by their negligence.

18. It is submitted that the standard of care expected from a medical professional is well-established by the Hon'ble
Supreme Court of India. In the landmark case of *Jacob Mathew v. State of Punjab*, (2005) 6 SCC 1, the Court held that a
professional may be held liable for negligence if he was not possessed of the requisite skill which he professed to
have, or if he did not exercise, with reasonable competence in the given case, the skill which he did possess. The
Defendant No. 1 has failed on both counts. The standard is not of the highest expert skill, but the "ordinary skill of
an ordinary competent man exercising that particular art." Causing injury to the bile duct during a routine
cholecystectomy is a deviation from this very standard.

19. The Defendant No. 2, XYZ Multispecialty Hospital, is vicariously liable for the negligent acts of its employee
and/or consultant, Defendant No. 1. The surgery was performed on the premises of the hospital, using its infrastructure,
and the Plaintiff had approached the hospital for treatment, which was then provided by the doctor empaneled with it.
The Hon'ble Supreme Court in *Savita Garg (Smt) v. Director, National Heart Institute*, (2004) 8 SCC 56, has clearly
established the principle of vicarious liability of hospitals for the negligence of their doctors, holding that a
hospital is responsible for the acts of its permanent and visiting doctors. The hospital's duty of care towards the
patient is non-delegable.

20. The negligence of the Defendants is the direct and proximate cause of the injuries and damages suffered by the Plaintiff. The chain of causation is unbroken: the negligent surgery led to the bile duct injury, which necessitated a second corrective surgery, which in turn led to additional medical expenses, immense physical pain, and mental trauma for the Plaintiff.

The principles for awarding compensation are rooted in the law of torts and are also reflected in statutory provisions such as Section 73 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872, which provides for compensation for loss or damage caused by a breach of contract. The said section reads as follows:
> **Section 73. Compensation for loss or damage caused by breach of contract.**—When a contract has been broken, the party who suffers by such breach is entitled to receive, from the party who has broken the contract, compensation for any loss or damage caused to him thereby, which naturally arose in the usual course of things from such breach, or which the parties knew, when they made the contract, to be likely to result from the breach of it.
