## Legal Reasoning and Case Law

12. It is submitted that Defendant No. 1, as the operating surgeon, and Defendant No. 2, as the hospital providing
medical services, owed a clear and undeniable duty of care to the Plaintiff. This duty required them to exercise the

Indian courts.

13. The Plaintiff submits that Defendant No. 1 committed a gross breach of this duty of care. Causing severe injury to
the common bile duct during a routine cholecystectomy is an act that falls far below the accepted standard of surgical
practice. It demonstrates a lack of reasonable care and skill in identifying and preserving the vital anatomical

performance of a professional duty.

14. The Plaintiff invokes the doctrine of *res ipsa loquitur* (the thing speaks for itself). The injury to the bile duct
is of a kind that does not ordinarily occur in the absence of negligence. The surgical instruments and the patient were
under the exclusive control of Defendant No. 1. Therefore, the very fact of the injury raises a presumption of
negligence, and the burden shifts to the Defendants to prove that the injury occurred without any negligence on their
part. The Supreme Court in *V. Kishan Rao v. Nikhil Super Speciality Hospital, (2010) 5 SCC 513*, held that in cases of
gross medical negligence, the principle of *res ipsa loquitur* can be applied, and the complainant is not required to
prove the negligence with precision.

15. It is further submitted that Defendant No. 2, XYZ Multispecialty Hospital, is vicariously liable for the negligent

whether they are on its permanent staff or are visiting consultants. The hospital has a duty to ensure that the doctors
it engages are competent and that they provide a proper standard of care.
